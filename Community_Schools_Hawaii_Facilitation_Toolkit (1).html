Our Facilitation Philosophy

Community Schools thrive because people work together—not because any one person has all the answers. Facilitation creates conditions for trust, belonging, collective learning, and shared responsibility.

Values in action

  • Aloha: Lead with care, dignity, reciprocity, and respect.
  • Collaboration: Honor expertise from families, students, staff, and partners.
  • Shared voice: Create multiple ways to influence discussion and decisions.
  • Growth: Treat meetings as opportunities to learn and improve.
  • Student- and family-centered: Ground decisions in lived experiences and outcomes.
A successful Hui is not simply a well-attended meeting. It is a space where people build trust, influence priorities, and take action together.
SECTION 02 / 10

Community Schools 101

What Is a Community School?

A Community School is both a place and a collaborative strategy: school leaders, educators, families, students, and community partners work together to align opportunities, relationships, and supports with locally identified strengths and needs.

It is not simply a school that offers services. The distinguishing feature is shared leadership and an ongoing process of listening, planning, implementing, and learning with the community.

What this looks like

  • Students and families help define priorities and solutions.
  • Schools and partners coordinate supports rather than working in isolation.
  • Learning opportunities connect to student interests and community assets.
  • Data and lived experience inform decisions and continuous improvement.

Learn more: Coalition for Community Schools · Learning Policy Institute.

Hawaiʻi Community Schools: 7 Key Practices

In Hawaiʻi, community schools are innovative, thriving hubs of learning in which partnerships and collaboration create aʻo mai, aʻo aku—reciprocal relationships between learners and place, content and context, and schools and communities.

Facilitated by the Community School Coordinator, the Community School Hui is a leadership team of school leaders, teachers, community partners, families, and youth. Together, they co-create the school’s community school action plan and co-design innovative solutions to improve student and family outcomes. Those solutions draw on one or more of these seven key practices.

1

Expanded, enriched learning opportunities

After-school and summer programs, extracurricular activities, and additional opportunities for learning and enrichment outside the school day.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Nita M. Lowey 21st Century Community Learning Centers; PALS and Places; UPLINK; student internships.

2

Rigorous, community-connected classroom instruction

Connecting classroom learning to the local community, students’ identities, and cultures.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Partnership with Pacific American Foundation to bring students to Waikalua Loko Iʻa during school; MAʻO Farms field trips; career exploration with local businesses.

3

Culture of belonging, safety, and care

School climate where students feel connected, welcomed, safe, and cared for.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Sign-waving to welcome students; student voice and representation in meetings; murals on campus made by students.

4

Powerful family and student engagement

Families and students are partners; provide opportunities and resources to families.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Home visits to talk with families about the importance of attendance; Family Resource Centers; Parent-Teacher Organizations; adult education opportunities and workshops.

5

Integrated systems of support

Wraparound services that address the basic needs of students and families.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Food pantry; Hawaiʻi Keiki nurse; day care in school.

6

Collaborative leadership, shared voice and power

Community School Hui, School Community Council, and other groups that facilitate collaborative leadership.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Community School Hui merged with School Community Council where appropriate and agreed upon.

7

Grounded in Hawaiʻi

Nā Hopena Aʻo and ʻāina-based learning opportunities.

Examples from the Hawaiʻi resource: Partnership with Kaʻala Farms; kūpuna teaching poi pounding, kapa making, and lei classes.

Use the practices in a Hui

  1. Invite members to identify where the school already has strengths across the seven practices.
  2. Discuss whose experiences or voices may be missing.
  3. Choose a priority and connect it to the needs-and-assets assessment and Community School Action Plan.
  4. Revisit progress at recurring Hui meetings.

Begin With Assets and Listen for Needs

Community Schools are grounded in the knowledge that every school community has strengths, relationships, histories, and resources. A strong assessment combines existing data with authentic student, family, staff, and partner perspectives.

Look across multiple sources

  • Student experiences, aspirations, and sense of belonging
  • Family priorities, access barriers, and cultural and linguistic assets
  • Attendance, academic, behavioral, and participation patterns
  • School and community organizations, programs, spaces, and relationships
Equity check: Whose voice is represented? Who may have difficulty attending or speaking? What structural conditions may be shaping the patterns we see?

Do not use individual students' identifiable records in broad Hui discussions. Share aggregate or appropriately de-identified information under applicable school policies.

SECTION 03 / 10

Understanding the Hui

What Is a Community School Hui?

A Community School Hui is a collaborative leadership space where school administrators, educators, students, families, and community partners help plan, implement, and assess the school's Community School strategy. It is a place to build relationships, elevate lived experience, coordinate resources, and shape collective action.

What a Hui can do

  • Develop a shared vision of student and family success.
  • Review school and community assets, needs, and data.
  • Co-design and refine the Community School Action Plan.
  • Coordinate partners, commitments, and follow-up.
  • Examine whether strategies are making a difference.

Who should be at the table?

School leadership, teachers, other staff, students, families, community partners, cultural practitioners, local businesses, health providers, higher education, faith organizations where appropriate, and district or state partners. Participation should reflect local priorities—not a fixed roster.

Ask regularly: Who is missing, and what would make it easier for them to participate meaningfully?

How the Hui Fits With Existing School Groups

Build on existing structures rather than automatically creating another committee. Roles and governance obligations vary by school.

GroupTypical focus
School Community Council (SCC)Advising on school improvement and academic planning under its established responsibilities
PTSAFamily-school connections, events, fundraising, and volunteer engagement
Parent Advisory Committee (PAC)Family perspectives, needs, and family-centered programming
Community School HuiCollaborative design, implementation, and improvement of the Community School strategy

An existing SCC or other group may be able to fulfill Hui functions when membership, agreement, and applicable governance requirements support that arrangement. The groups are not automatically interchangeable.

Three Stages of Hui Development

1 · Launch

Build relationships, understand Community Schools, co-create a shared vision, and broaden participation.

2 · Plan

Review assets and needs, select priorities, develop the Community School Action Plan, and clarify responsibilities.

3 · Sustain

Implement strategies, monitor progress, learn from evidence and community voice, and adjust together.

Hui do not move through these stages only once. Changes in membership, school priorities, or community circumstances may require revisiting visioning or planning.

SECTION 04 / 10

Plan a Hui

Plan With Intention

Three grounding questions

  1. Why? What needs to happen—relationships, learning, brainstorming, decisions, or action planning?
  2. Who? Whose expertise, experiences, and decision-making authority are needed?
  3. What? What should people know or receive in advance?

Before

  • Agree on a clear purpose and 1–2 desired outcomes.
  • Invite participants early using accessible communication channels.
  • Check timing, parking, transportation, childcare considerations, language access, physical accessibility, technology, and food.
  • Prepare agenda, data, materials, name tags, and a note-taking plan.

During

  • Welcome and connect; explain the purpose and how input will be used.
  • Use multiple participation formats and protect discussion time.
  • Record decisions, differing perspectives, and concrete commitments.

After

  • Send a concise summary to attendees and those absent.
  • Track owners, deadlines, and supports needed.
  • Follow up between meetings, celebrate progress, and invite additional voices.

Shared Leadership

From Attendance to Shared Ownership

Inviting community members is only a starting point. Meaningful shared leadership creates opportunities to influence the agenda, identify priorities, make recommendations, shape decisions, and assess progress.

Participation

Attend, listen, volunteer, share feedback.

Shared leadership

Co-design agendas, facilitate, help decide, and own actions together.

Equity and accessibility questions

  • Who is regularly heard? Who has decision-making influence?
  • Can students and families lead—not just respond?
  • Do time, transportation, language, technology, and power dynamics limit participation?
  • Are roles voluntary, supported, and realistically sized?

Share responsibilities and influence. Asking a family member to bring food is helpful; inviting that family member to shape a priority is a different, equally important opportunity.

Suggested Hui Leadership Roles

Roles are flexible and may be rotated or combined. A coordinator can support continuity without owning every task.

RoleContribution
CoordinatorCoordinates invitations, agendas, communication, and continuity
GreeterWelcomes members and helps create belonging
Community Builder LeadPlans a short connection activity
Discussion Facilitator / SpeakerGuides dialogue and supports equitable participation
Note TakerCaptures themes, decisions, and commitments
Outreach / RecruiterInvites families, students, and community partners
Hospitality / Preparation LeadHelps with setup, refreshments, and accessibility
Action Follow-Up LeadChecks progress and helps remove barriers

Grow leadership over time

Start small, invite based on interests, offer co-facilitation and preparation, rotate roles, and acknowledge contributions. Do not equate volunteering with consent to ongoing obligations.

Invite People In—and Make It Possible to Stay

Outreach practices

  • Use personal invitations alongside newsletters, email, texts, and school channels.
  • Explain why someone's perspective matters and how input will influence action.
  • Ask participants who else should be included.
  • Offer interpretation, translated materials, accessible formats, and hybrid options when feasible.
  • Choose times and spaces that work for families and students—not only staff schedules.

Retention practices

  • Open with a welcoming ritual and relationship-building.
  • Share what changed because of prior feedback.
  • Follow up personally when appropriate.
  • Celebrate contributions without pressuring people to participate.
A useful invitation: “We are working together to improve ____. Your perspective could help us understand what matters most. Would you like to join or share your ideas another way?”

Facilitation Skills

Facilitate With Curiosity and Care

A facilitator guides the process without needing to control the answer. Explain the purpose, protect time, surface diverse perspectives, summarize accurately, and help the group move toward next steps.

Suggested Hui agreements

  • Listen to understand; share the space.
  • Lead with aloha and respect different perspectives.
  • Stay curious; question ideas without blaming people.
  • Center students and families.
  • Protect confidentiality and honor commitments.
  • Begin and end on time.

Co-create or revisit norms with participants rather than assuming agreement. “Assume positive intent” can be replaced with “Attend to intent and impact” when that better supports accountability.

Ask Questions That Open Possibility

To understand

“What are we noticing?” · “What have students and families told us?” · “What else might explain this pattern?”

To build on assets

“What strengths already exist?” · “Who is already doing promising work?” · “What have we tried?”

To make decisions

“What criteria matter most?” · “Whose experience might be affected?” · “What is one small step?”

To invite more voices

“Let's take one minute to write first.” · “Would anyone who hasn't spoken like to add something?” · “What perspectives are we missing?”

Participation techniques

  • Silent writing before open discussion
  • Pairs or small groups before whole-group sharing
  • Round-robin with an option to pass
  • Sticky notes or anonymous input when appropriate
  • Summarize, check understanding, then invite correction

Parking Lot

Write valuable but off-purpose topics on a visible sheet. Before closing, decide which will be revisited, by whom, and when.

Consensus check

Thumbs up: can support moving forward. Sideways: need clarification or changes. Down: cannot support yet. Invite concerns; don't treat a quick vote as a substitute for established decision rules.

How to Facilitate Brainstorming

1. Prepare

Define the challenge, choose a technique, gather materials and establish no-judgment norms.

2. Open

Warm up; state the question, purpose and time limit.

3. Generate

Invite ideas using silent writing, round robin with a pass option, mind mapping or breakout groups.

4. Capture and cluster

Record suggestions visibly; group similar ideas without prematurely rejecting them.

5. Prioritize and follow up

Use Impact–Effort or another agreed decision process; assign next steps.

Choose a technique

Silent brainstorming: equitable thinking time. Round robin: structured invitations. Mind mapping: connections among ideas. SWOT: strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats. Six Thinking Hats: perspectives method developed by Edward de Bono.

Facilitator prompt: “How might we build on what is already working in our community?”

Photovoice: Seeing Community Through Its Members

Participants document community strengths, concerns and hopes through photographs and their own stories. The goal is to elevate lived experience and inform action—not merely to collect pictures.

  1. Co-define the purpose with students and families.
  2. Invite participants and explain the project, timelines and voluntary nature.
  3. Set consent, privacy and safety agreements; follow school rules for photographing minors and publishing images.
  4. Invite photos of strengths, barriers or opportunities; offer non-photo alternatives.
  5. Facilitate participant-led discussion about what images mean; consult the original guide for the SHOWED method.
  6. Cluster themes, choose actions, and link to the Needs & Assets Assessment.
  7. Share only with permission, and report back on what changed.

Short Hui version: Invite one appropriate photo of a community strength, then discuss possible actions. A one-meeting adaptation is not a full Photovoice project.

SECTION 07 / 10

Collaborative Action

A Repeatable Problem-Solving Pathway

1 · Identify a shared priority

Listen to student and family voice; examine assets and needs.

2 · Explore contributing factors

Use the 5 Whys or another root-cause discussion without blaming individuals.

3 · Generate options

Brainstorm solutions and existing partnerships.

4 · Prioritize

Use the Impact–Effort Matrix, while considering equity and long-term needs.

5 · Commit and learn

Agree on owners, timelines, evidence of progress, and a review date.

Data discussion protocol

  1. Observe: What do we see? Separate facts from interpretations.
  2. Interpret: What might explain it? Whose perspective is missing?
  3. Act: What will we try, and how will we know if it helps?

5 Whys: Explore Before Solving

Use this tool when the group has named a specific challenge but needs to better understand contributing factors. The number five is a guide, not a requirement.

Facilitation steps · 15–20 minutes

  1. Gather people with different perspectives on the issue.
  2. Agree on a specific, observable problem statement.
  3. Ask “Why might this be happening?” and record evidence-based responses.
  4. Follow each plausible branch with additional “why” questions.
  5. Stop when further questions no longer add useful insight; identify what still needs checking.
  6. Co-design responses that address contributing conditions.
  7. Review whether the response actually helped.

Example: Attendance

Some students miss school → transportation is inconsistent → routes or schedules do not meet family needs → families lack reliable alternatives → the Hui explores communication, transportation supports, and partnerships. Illustrative only: do not presume the cause of actual absences.

Use “What conditions are contributing?” instead of repeatedly asking a person “Why did you do that?” A single issue can have multiple causes.

Read: How to Use the 5 Whys — MindTools · Cause and Effect Analysis.

Impact–Effort Matrix: Choose Together

Use after generating potential strategies. The Hui—not the facilitator alone—should define what “impact” and “effort” mean locally.

Quick WinsHigh impact · Low effort

Prioritize and try soon.

Major ProjectsHigh impact · High effort

Plan, partner, and resource.

Fill-In ActivitiesLow impact · Low effort

Consider if capacity allows.

ReconsiderLow impact · High effort

Redesign or defer.

← Lower effort       Higher effort →

How to facilitate · 30–40 minutes

  1. Brainstorm ideas on separate sticky notes.
  2. Agree on criteria for impact (student/family benefit, equity, reach) and effort (time, funding, coordination, staffing).
  3. Place ideas on the matrix collaboratively.
  4. Discuss disagreements and test assumptions against evidence.
  5. Choose next steps and assign responsibility.

Important: Do not automatically reject high-effort work. Systemic, high-impact changes may be essential. The placement of any initiative depends on local context; tutoring or a community garden is not universally “low effort.”

Root Cause Analysis Using RBA and the 5 Whys

Begin with a shared result and a verified baseline. Illustrative training scenario: “70% of families say they do not feel connected to their student's school.” This is not an actual school statistic.

  1. Assemble a group that includes people experiencing the issue; identify a facilitator.
  2. Agree on a specific problem statement and evidence.
  3. Ask “Why?” and capture more than one possible branch.
  4. Ask why again for each branch, as many times as helpful; five is a guideline.
  5. Verify hypotheses with families, students and data rather than assuming causes.
  6. Agree on changes to test and who will lead them.
  7. Monitor outcomes and adjust.

Possible explanations to investigate, not assume

Are school interactions mostly transactional? Do families feel welcome? Are they invited to decisions or only events? Are outreach time, language, transportation or childcare barriers involved? Have families been asked how they want to engage?

RBA framing: What result do we want? What does the baseline show? What is the story behind the data? Who are our partners? What works to turn the curve?

“So what?” appreciation: a complementary way to explore implications of a fact rather than its causes.

Continuous Improvement

Continuous Improvement Is a Habit of Learning

Continuous improvement means using evidence and community experience to understand what is happening, try changes, and adjust over time. It is not a one-time evaluation or an expectation of perfection.

1 · Build shared vision and trust

Define what thriving looks like and create space for learning.

2 · Select indicators and baseline

Agree on meaningful outcomes and a starting point.

3 · Gather and interpret evidence

Use quantitative data, feedback, and observations through kilo.

4 · Plan and implement

Set goals, define roles, and try strategies.

5 · Monitor, reflect, adjust

Celebrate progress, notice unintended effects, and adapt.

Not every meeting needs to cover the full cycle. Connect meetings over time through consistent notes, action trackers, and regular reviews.

Related locally shared guide: Guide to Facilitating Continuous Improvement at Community Schools (original link pending).

Three Questions for Reviewing Strategies

How much did we do?

Quantity or reach: number of events, students served, or partner contacts.

How well did we do it?

Quality and access: participation experience, timeliness, consistency, and inclusion.

Is anyone better off?

Meaningful outcomes: connection, attendance, confidence, access, or other agreed changes.

Use multiple forms of evidence

Combine baseline trends with feedback from students and families, staff experiences, and thoughtful observation. Avoid collecting data simply because it is easy to count.

Questions for the Hui

  • What changed, and for whom?
  • What is still unclear?
  • Could other factors explain the change?
  • What should we keep, stop, adapt, or try next?

After Every Hui: Reflect and Improve

  • Did participants feel welcomed and respected?
  • Did students and families have meaningful opportunities to contribute?
  • Did we stay connected to our shared purpose?
  • Did we use evidence responsibly and protect privacy?
  • Did we leave with clear decisions and voluntary commitments?
  • Did we strengthen relationships and shared ownership?
  • What will I do differently next time?

Keep a short learning log

Worked well: ____   Could improve: ____   Next experiment: ____

Progress is better than perfection. Celebrate small wins, learn openly, and keep returning to student and family experience.

Guide to Facilitating Continuous Improvement

1 · Build a culture of improvement

Shared vision, collaboration and learning from setbacks.

2 · Identify indicators and baseline

Define success and starting measures such as attendance, belonging and family engagement.

3 · Collect and analyze data

Use records, surveys, feedback and kilo; share accessible aggregate findings.

4 · Set SMART goals and action plans

Prioritize, write measurable goals, name leads and supports.

5 · Implement and monitor

Try strategies, track implementation and celebrate progress.

6 · Evaluate and adapt

Compare with baseline, gather perspectives and adjust or pivot.

At each recurring Hui

Ask: How much did we do? How well did we do it? Is anyone better off? Then decide what to keep, stop, improve or try next. Do not assume a strategy caused a change without considering other factors.
